Openpi Comet:
what reproduces, and what stops you

Team Comet took 2nd place in the 2025 BEHAVIOR Challenge and released everything — code, checkpoints, and their post-training dataset. This is an attempt to re-derive their results, and an account of the hardware constraint that decides who can try.

Reproduction isn't one thing. It's five claims whose costs span four orders of magnitude — and knowing which is which is the first real result.

Two things fall out before any GPU is touched. The inference-time ablations are the highest-value target — they're pure runtime flags, so released checkpoints make them testable with no training at all. And the released RFT dataset lets me interrogate the post-training claim — the one I categorically cannot re-run — for free.

I ran both. The free one finished and produced the sharpest result on this page.

FINDING

Why this is a finding, not a footnote

It explains a design decision in the paper. §4.3 rejects online RL partly because it needs a heterogeneous setup — RT Cores for simulation, Tensor Cores for training. The authors are describing this exact constraint.

"online RL requires a heterogeneous compute setup: GPUs with RT Cores are needed for simulation, while GPUs with Tensor Cores are needed for model training and rollouts."

— Openpi Comet, §4.3

Read that way, RFT isn't only a sample-efficiency choice. It's a choice that decouples training and simulation in time, so you never need both GPU classes at once. That's the most transferable idea in the report, and it sits in one sentence.

And the constraint is structural. Checking the ACCESS-CI catalog: Delta's A40 nodes are the only clearly RT-capable GPUs in the entire inventory. DeltaAI, Vista, Lonestar6, Bridges-2, Expanse, Anvil, ACES — none can render a frame of this benchmark. The national GPU buildout optimised for tensor-core throughput, and embodied-AI simulation fell outside it.

E4

What the released RFT dataset says

Executed. Zero compute. The one experiment I could run against the claim I could never re-run.

Hypothesis

The paper attributes its 0.224 → 0.345 improvement to a refined "task balancing" strategy when selecting 1,469 trajectories from ~25,500 rollouts. If balancing drove the gain, the kept set should be more uniform across tasks than raw rejection sampling would produce.

Method

Pulled meta/episodes.jsonl from delinqu/comet-1.5k; computed per-task episode counts, per-task mean horizon, and the rank correlation between them.

Episodes retained per task, sorted — 39 tasks

"Task balancing" is a cap, not a rebalance

Five tasks sit at exactly 120 — a flat ceiling. Over-represented tasks get capped; rare tasks are not lifted. The distribution stays 120:1 skewed, the top five tasks hold 40.8% of the data, and the median task contributes twelve episodes.

FINDING 02

Yield collapses with horizon

Spearman ρ(episodes kept, mean episode length) = −0.45. Tasks with ≥100 episodes kept average 4.1 minutes; everything else averages ~9. Rejection sampling harvests almost entirely from the short half of the benchmark.

FINDING 03

Eleven tasks yielded nothing

39 of 50 tasks appear. Roughly 25,500 rollouts produced zero retained successes on the other eleven — RFT is silent exactly where the benchmark is hardest.

FINDING 04

Native resolution is 720 / 480

Head 720×720, wrist 480×480 — exactly Table 3's "high resolution" row. The ablation's 2× gain isn't from adding information.

FINDING 05

A stale frame count

info.json reports 12,226,350 frames; summing episode lengths gives 14,896,614 — an 18% gap, likely a count not regenerated after final filtering. Flagged, not load-bearing.

Where this diverges from the paper

The paper credits task balancing. The artifact shows balancing did comparatively little — the distribution stays extremely skewed after it. What the data actually shows is a horizon-dependent sampling bias.

That sharpens the paper's own conclusion. It admits "sampling efficiency is still too low." The data says something stronger: RFT cannot close the gap to the reported 0.611 theoretical best by construction, because it generates no signal at all on the eleven tasks that need it most.

A second reframing

Finding 04 recasts Table 3's resolution result. The 0.30 → 0.60 gain is not from adding information — it's from ceasing to discard it, since 224×224 downsamples data that is natively 720/480. "High-fidelity perception is essential" is more honestly stated as "don't downsample your data."

E3

The radio probe

The paper hands you the ideal test and doesn't take it. Every measurement here is on turning_on_radio — and the choice isn't ours, it's theirs.

WHY THIS TASK

It's their own probe

Every cell of Table 3 and Table 4 was measured on it. Using the same task removes the "different model, different task" confound entirely.

WHY THIS TASK

They claim 100%

Fig 4 reports it at 1.00 and the caption says the policy performs it "robustly". That's an extreme, falsifiable prediction — not a vague directional claim.

WHY THIS TASK

It's the cheapest in the benchmark

934 frames = 31 s, 2.5× shorter than the next task. Cheapest possible test of the strongest possible claim.

We can afford twice their sample size

The public instance list holds 20 IDs for this task, but the eval harness defaults to NUM_EVAL_INSTANCES = 10. That's why every number in Fig 4 and Table 3 lands on a multiple of 0.1. The finding that doesn't need the runs to finish

Computing confidence intervals on the paper's own numbers surfaces something it never states. Their most-quoted ablation — that native resolution doubles success, 0.30 → 0.60 — is not statistically resolvable at their sample size, or at twice their sample size:

n0.30 → 95% CI0.60 → 95% CISeparable?
10 (theirs)[0.11, 0.60][0.31, 0.83]OVERLAP
20 (ours)[0.15, 0.52][0.39, 0.78]OVERLAP

The same applies to Table 4's null results: "skill weighting yields no improvement" is indistinguishable from "we could not have detected one." Several reported effects in this paper are point estimates from ten trials, presented without intervals.

Pre-registered predictions

Committed before running, so they can't be fitted afterwards. H1 — temporal ensemble and receding temporal collapse toward zero. H2 — native resolution beats 224×224. H3′ — shorter executed chunks degrade control.

I expect H2 to hold and H1 to weaken. H2 has independent support from E4's finding 04: if the effect is really "stop downsampling," it should transfer across checkpoints. H1's mechanism is entangled with how a single-task model's action distribution behaves — exactly what multi-task training changes.

Ran at 224×224. Every instance ended at exactly 4300 simulator steps with q_score.final = 0.000:

At face value: Fig 4's 100% claim does not reproduce. That would have been a false claim about someone else's work. Config B is the tell — it agrees with the paper's 0.00, and that agreement is worthless, because a harness incapable of producing any success returns 0.00 for every configuration, matching every prediction of zero it is tested against.

The control that caught it

Running the same policy on training instances it had already been fitted to:

POSCONTROL (train, 224)     n=6   nonzero=0   meanQ=0.000   -> harness suspect
DIAGNOSTIC (train, native)  n=4   nonzero=1   meanQ=0.250   -> pipeline discriminates

Zero on data the model was trained on is not a policy failing to generalise — it is a dead instrument. The cause was mine. Upstream defaults env_wrapper to RGBWrapper (native 720/480) and the README recommends it for evaluation; I overrode it to 224 so the baseline would match Table 3's row. That reasoning was wrong — Table 3's 224 numbers come from a single-task SFT model trained at 224. The released multi-task checkpoint does not degrade at 224, it fails completely.

This control was not in the original design. Any experiment whose predicted outcome is zero cannot separate a confirmed hypothesis from a broken apparatus without one.

H2 is unverifiable — for two independent reasons

The resolution arm is dead. The 224 condition cannot be measured on this checkpoint at all, and reproducing Table 3 #4 would need the per-resolution single-task models, which were never released. So the paper's most-quoted ablation — native resolution doubles success, 0.30 → 0.60 — cannot be checked because (1) the artifacts do not exist publicly, and (2) even with them, the intervals overlap at n=10 and at n=20.

● Corrected sweep running

Native resolution throughout: baseline, then the two control-mode arms, then the chunk-length variants. Results land in the repo's experiment-design.md §9.5.

Isolating the cause — three hypotheses, two eliminated

The first version of this page claimed a measured Blackwell incompatibility. That claim was premature: I attributed a crash to GPU architecture before ruling out the environment, and both machines were failing for a shared reason that had nothing to do with GPUs. The corrected chain:

With numpy pinned on both, and each machine running a torch build that supports its own architecture, everything else identical:

RTX 3090  sm_86    numpy 1.26.4  torch 2.6.0+cu124 (sm_86 ok)    og.launch() -> rc=0

That is the controlled result — one variable, two confounds eliminated first. The lesson for me was methodological: a crash reproducing on two machines is evidence of a shared cause, not of the difference between them.

DEFECT

setup.sh breaks its own numpy pin

setup.sh installs numpy 1.26.4 as OmniGibson requires — then the JoyLo dependency chain (mediapipe, mujoco, dm_control) silently re-upgrades it to 2.2.6, breaking the ABI for every pybind11 extension built against 1.x.

Anyone running ./setup.sh --joylo --eval on a clean machine gets a simulator that segfaults with no usable error. On the 3090 it surfaces first as TypeError: Unable to write from unknown dtype, kind=f, size=0. This cost roughly two hours and is worth reporting upstream.

The competition code was written against v3.7.x. Upstream removed omnigibson/learning/ by 3.9, so using a Blackwell-capable stack means abandoning the evaluation harness you are trying to reproduce. This is not a bug in anyone's code — it is what happens when a benchmark moves faster than the work built on it, and it makes a nine-month-old competition entry materially harder to re-run than its "everything is released" framing suggests.

Worth separating clearly: the policy half is unaffected. JAX 0.5.3 enumerates the RTX 5090, selects the GPU backend, and executes correctly. The constraint is entirely on the simulation side.

Challenges & insights

01

The constraint was hardware class, not quantity

Compute planning for embodied AI needs a capability filter (RT Cores) before a capacity one. Aggregate VRAM is the wrong metric when the simulator gates you.

02

The cheapest experiment was the most informative

E4 cost zero GPU-hours and produced the sharpest divergence. When the expensive claim can't be re-run, interrogate the artifact it produced. That generalises well past this paper.

03

Released ≠ reproducible

The README calls the same checkpoints both "base models ideal for fine-tuning" (the 0.192 stage) and "our latest models with Q Score of 0.345." Both can't be true, and a reader can't tell which backs the headline without spending GPU-hours.

04

Queue depth is an experimental variable

Delta's A40 partition had 1,486 jobs queued and zero idle nodes — a ~5 hour wait. On a deadline, that mattered more than throughput. Size experiments to the scheduler you have.

05

Documentation drifts faster than hardware

My own notes had b3iq as a Windows box needing WSL2 — the single biggest risk I'd priced. It had been rebuilt as native Ubuntu 24.04. Verify the machine, don't trust the note.

06

What the paper gets right

Choosing RFT over online RL because of the RT/Tensor-core split is genuinely good systems reasoning — and it's buried in a single sentence.

If I had more time

  • Finish E3, then run E1 (per-task eval against Fig 4) — which would also settle the checkpoint ambiguity above.
  • E2: pt12 vs pt50, testing the pre-training-coverage claim on released artifacts with no training.
